**<u>Why this dashboard, and why now?</u>**

The Loi Solidarité et Renouvellement Urbains (SRU) is turning twenty‑five. Adopted in 2000 and reinforced in 2013, it has been one of the most ambitious and constraining pieces of housing legislation in contemporary France. Its core idea is simple. Every commune should take its share in the national effort to build social housing. In practice, this has meant confronting deeply rooted territorial inequalities and local political resistance.

When SRU was introduced in 2000, France was emerging from a period of stagnant housing production and rapidly rising costs. Two decades later, the structural crisis has not disappeared. The Mouvement HLM (Union of social housing authorities) now estimates that close to 3 million households are waiting for a home that fits their needs[1], while social landlords, under tighter financial constraints and rising construction costs, expect to produce around 100,000 new units and renovate 125,000 dwellings in 2025—an effort that remains below the scale of need.

Yet, despite the law’s centrality in public debates on housing, its real workings remain largely opaque to non‑specialists. The official data produced by the Ministry of Housing are complex, scattered, and rarely made fully legible to journalists, activists, or citizens. SRU is often talked about in generalities \[“good idea, poor implementation” or “overly bureaucratic and punitive”\] without an accessible, detailed view of who has built what, where, and for whom over the last quarter century.

This project, *“The Loi SRU, 25 Years Later”*, starts from a simple conviction. To defend SRU against its detractors, we need to demystify it. The dashboard makes the Ministry’s dataset fully available and explorable through interactive maps, time‑series graphs, and profiles for each commune. It allows users to see, at a glance and in detail, how the law has reshaped the geography of social housing, how sanctions have been applied, and how local political choices have steered outcomes. It also situates SRU within a broader French social housing ecosystem which, with more than five million units and a national target of at least 25% social housing in eligible municipalities[2], has become one of the few in Europe where the share of social housing has actually increased since the early 2000s, albeit with major territorial and social imbalances still unresolved.

We situate this work explicitly in the “SRU at 25” moment. The law has already been the object of significant reforms, notably through the 2022 3DS law (différenciation, décentralisation, déconcentration, simplification).[3] The 3DS cycle is now behind us, but it offers a cautionary tale. Even when SRU is formally prolonged, reforms can quietly chip away at its foundations and dilute its redistributive ambitions. This dashboard is therefore both a research tool and a political instrument: a way to see clearly what is at stake when the law is weakened, and to argue for strengthening, rather than hollowing out, its core principles.

**<u>What the SRU Law does (and does not do)</u>**

At its heart, the SRU law sets quantitative and qualitative obligations for social housing at the level of each eligible commune.

***Quantitative obligations:***

Communes above certain size thresholds, and belonging to specific intercommunal areas, are required to reach minimum percentages of social housing in their total housing stock. These obligations are evaluated over multi‑year (triennial) periods: each commune must finance or deliver a minimum number of social housing units every three years, with the aim of converging towards the legal target share. When communes fall short, they may be declared “deficient”, and can face reinforced obligations and financial sanctions.

Importantly, the law recognizes that “producing” social housing can take several forms: not only new construction but also the acquisition and improvement of existing buildings, their transfer into the social housing stock, and rehabilitation or restructuring operations. From the early triennial assessments onwards, categories such as logements ANAH conventionnés appear alongside PLAI/PLUS/PLS[4], signalling that SRU’s toolbox includes both new build and the transformation of the existing stock.

***Qualitative obligations:***

SRU does not treat all social housing the same. The law distinguishes three broad categories of financing:

-   PLS (Prêt Locatif Social): aimed primarily at middle and upper‑middle‑income households;

-   PLUS (Prêt Locatif à Usage Social): the “standard” social housing product, targeting low‑ and middle‑income households;

-   PLAI (Prêt Locatif Aidé d’Intégration): very social housing, for households in situations of high precarity.

SRU requires a balanced mix of these categories. A minimum share of PLAI (very social) units must be produced, while the proportion of PLS is capped (with lower caps in some communes), to avoid a situation where “social housing” is used mainly to accommodate relatively better‑off households. The law therefore contains, at least in principle, a strong distributive intention. It ensures that the most precarious households are not systematically excluded from new social housing supply. This principle has been both reinforced and threatened over time by national debates and evaluations, which regularly underline that the majority of households on waiting lists fall under PLAI or PLUS resource ceilings, and that the most “useful” marginal unit is often the one that can house the very low‑income households who otherwise remain stuck in overcrowded or poor‑quality private rentals.

***Sanctions and Article 55:***

When a commune persistently fails to meet its obligations, Article 55 introduces financial penalties. These can take the form of increased contributions to a national solidarity fund, or of reinforced controls over local planning decisions. In theory, the combination of binding targets, deadlines, and sanctions is what makes SRU more than a rhetorical commitment. It acts as a legal framework designed to produce real change on the ground. In some cases, repeated non‑compliance can even trigger a partial “take‑over” of permitting powers by the State, echoing what comparative analysts have described as one of SRU’s distinctive features: a strong architecture of carrots (subsidized loans, tax advantages, dedicated funds) combined with credible sticks (penalties that may reach several percent of the operating budget, building permit freeze) to ensure that every commune contributes.

What SRU does not do, however, is guarantee by itself that social housing will be fairly distributed across the national territory. The law sets constraints and incentives, but it operates within a field shaped by local politics, land markets, and long histories of investment and disinvestment. Understanding where SRU has succeeded and where it has failed requires looking closely at this geography. This is precisely what the dashboard seeks to make visible.

**<u>Twenty‑five years of SRU: achievements and blind spots</u>**

From a national perspective, SRU’s impact is undeniably significant. Since the early 2000s, the social housing stock has increased markedly, from roughly 4.1 million units to nearly 5 million.[5] Peaks of construction, such as in the mid‑2010s, have been sufficient at times to surpass national objectives, and some triennial periods have even exceeded the targeted number of new social housing units.

Over the same period, however, other indicators have moved in the opposite direction. National monitoring by agencies such as ANCOLS shows that housing demand, formalized through applications for social housing, has continued to rise, particularly in hot housing markets like Île‑de‑France, where Paris is located, which alone concentrates close to a million active applications, nearly 28% of national demand. Meanwhile, annual allocations of social housing have fallen from about 467,000 in 2018 to around 418,000 in 2022[6], a 10% drop over five years, driven by slower turnover and a weaker net increase in the stock (fewer new units and more sales). The consequence is a declining rate of attribution, especially acute in the regions where SRU obligations are the most critical.

The dashboard’s time‑series tools make these trends visible at multiple scales, from France as a whole, down to the individual commune. Users can track the evolution of social housing stocks and the pace of new approvals. These visualizations confirm that SRU has contributed to a real expansion of the social housing sector.

But the same data also reveal deep territorial imbalances and persistent blind spots:

-   A relatively small number of communes, often already heavily invested in social housing, concentrate a very large share of new construction. Paris and a handful of communes in Île‑de‑France, as well as certain regional hubs, carry a disproportionate part of the national effort.

-   Around two decades into SRU’s implementation, approximately 70% of new social housing had been built in communes that already hosted significant concentrations of poverty, often in or around quartiers prioritaires de la politique de la ville (QPV). In other words, the law has not fully broken the logic of concentrating precarious households in the same territories. Today, QPV represent only about 7% of all primary residences in France, but roughly 65% of the housing located in these neighborhoods is social housing.[7] This means that the issue is not whether social housing exists outside priority neighborhoods (it does) but rather how new supply is distributed between already highly social‑housing‑dominated areas and wealthier, under‑equipped communes that continue to resist.

-   Many wealthier, often more conservative communes have systematically under‑complied. Some “play the rules” by favoring small units, specific target groups (students, seniors, the military) or peripheral locations that do not really advance long‑term social mix. Others simply accept to pay recurrent fines rather than alter their local housing model.

Recent research echoes my hypothesis and sheds light on the electoral calculus that can underlie these choices. The IDHEAL report *Maires bâtisseurs, maires battus ?* (2026) finds that[8], between 2014 and 2020, the effects of building on electoral outcomes are heterogeneous: in small communes, “bâtisseurs” were often more likely to be reelected, whereas in large cities (over 50,000 or 100,000 inhabitants) mayors associated with strong housing growth were more exposed to electoral backlash, reinforcing the well‑known intuition that, in big cities, “maire bâtisseur, maire battu” can become a self‑fulfilling fear. The same work also documents partisan differences in housing dynamism and in the growth of the social stock: large left‑led cities tended to increase their social housing stock more than comparable right‑led ones, even though there is no simple aggregate left/right gap for smaller communes. In other words, the geography of SRU is also the geography of political risk, NIMBY mobilization, and partisan strategies. These patterns appear clearly in the dashboard’s municipality profiles and maps. Users can filter by:

-   Level of compliance and deficit,

-   Political color of the mayoral administration,

-   Socio‑economic indicators,

-   Health outcomes and quality‑of‑life metrics in areas that have absorbed the most social housing over time.

Taken together, these indicators point to a key conclusion from my research: national constraints matter, but they are not enough. The effectiveness of SRU depends on what might be called local “land use ideologies”, as in the ways in which mayors and municipal coalitions imagine the future of their territory, who they consider “desirable” residents, and how they choose to mobilise or protect their land and planning tools.

In communes where the mayor and their allies embrace social mix and redistribution, SRU can be a powerful lever: sanctions and targets give them arguments and resources to build, even against local opposition. Where local political elites equate social housing with decline or ghettoization, sanctions alone rarely suffice to overcome obstruction. The dashboard’s data is, in this sense, a cartography of these land use ideologies, as much as it is a record of housing output. It therefore acts as a reminder that planning and housing policies are not neutral technocratic processes, but deeply political arenas where access to land and housing is unevenly mediated.

**<u>Methods and stance of this project</u>**

This dashboard builds on several years of research into the effects of SRU and, more specifically, into the operation of Article 55 sanctions on non‑compliant communes. The quantitative backbone of the project is the until now confidential Ministry dataset on social housing stocks, approvals, and SRU obligations, which has been re‑processed to be navigable at multiple geographic levels and over the full twenty‑five years of implementation.

***Data and analytical approach:***

The core operations behind the dashboard include:

-   Standardizing Ministry data on social housing stocks and flows, by commune and by triennial period, and aligning them with political, socio‑economic, and health indicators.

-   Distinguishing PLAI, PLUS, and PLS in both existing stock and new production, to evaluate not just “how much” social housing is built, but for whom.

-   Tracking sanctions and deficits under Article 55, identifying communes that are recurrently “deficient” or that oscillate between compliance and non‑compliance.

-   Developing econometric models to compare the evolution of social housing stocks before and after SRU, and to estimate the differential impact of the law across types of territories and political configurations.

Concretely, the SRU component of the dashboard rests on a series of detailed Excel tables transmitted by the Ministry in charge of housing[9], covering all triennial bilan reports from 2002 to 2022 as well as annual “bilan SRU” files. These tables bring together, for each commune, the SRU perimeter to which it is attached (EPCI or unité urbaine), the applicable obligation rate, the social housing stock used as a reference, the number of units financed or delivered during each period by type of financing, and the level of financial penalty. The dataset is not static over time. The number and identity of listed communes change as some cross the demographic thresholds that make them eligible, others fall below those thresholds, and many are transformed by the creation of new communes through mergers in the 2010s. Part of the work behind the dashboard has therefore consisted in harmonizing these moving perimeters (tracking mergers, entries and exits from the SRU universe, and occasional reporting inconsistencies) to build a consolidated longitudinal master table where each line corresponds to a commune (in its current configuration) observed over successive periods. The Ministry’s own team has documented some remaining caveats. Pre‑2020 data can still contain a small number of local input errors. SRU monitoring in the DROM is hampered by less reliable counts of primary residences[10] and by specific legal exemptions (for example, Mahoran communes in deficit are exempt from financial penalties). Some sub stocks (such as certain senior residences) are only partially captured. In general, all dwellings eligible for rental assistance (APL), including most senior housing within the social system, EHPAD, résidences autonomie and some forms of inclusive housing, are counted in the SRU inventory, whereas purely market‑oriented senior service residences are not.

On top of this quantitative work, the project incorporates qualitative fieldwork and case studies, particularly in Paris and in selected regional and overseas territories. These case studies inform the “housing regime” typologies presented in the Post-Occupancy Evaluation page of this dashboard, and help interpret the patterns made visible by mapping and data analysis.

***A data activism methodology:***

This is not a “neutral” visualization of housing data. The project takes a clear stance. It’s built to defend the principle of SRU as a tool of territorial justice, and to criticize right-wing and extreme-right-wing reform propositions that undermine this goal. Being explicit about this position is, in my view, part of methodological rigor. The dashboard does not hide behind the illusion of purely technical objectivity. Instead, it offers transparent access to the same data that informs national debates, and invites users to form their own interpretations. At the same time, it is organized to foreground questions that are often sidelined in official reporting:

-   Which communes have consistently refused to welcome low‑income households, despite legal obligations?

-   How do health and quality‑of‑life indicators evolve in areas that have taken on a disproportionate share of the social housing effort?

This project also treats the data infrastructure around housing as a public good in its own right. Housing laws like SRU can only be implemented and enforced if the underlying data is robust, accessible, and interpretable by those who need it (that’s you: national administrations, local mayoral administrations, social landlords, but also journalists, tenant organizers, and residents). In many contexts, we build tools that are too complex for citizens yet too simplistic for professionals, or we imagine “the public” as direct users when we should design for intermediaries too, as in tenant and community organizers. The dashboard tries to respond to this by clarifying who its audiences are, making expert indicators explorable through intuitive interfaces, and avoiding purely one‑off visualizations that would quickly become obsolete. Universities and research centers are natural hosts for such infrastructure. This project is a small contribution to that broader ecosystem.

Mentioning the 3DS reform here is important. Although the law formally extended SRU beyond its original 2025 horizon, it also introduced mechanisms that risk normalizing the idea of ceilings on social housing in communes that already exceed certain thresholds (typically 40%), without establishing equally strong constraints on recalcitrant, under‑equipped municipalities. This episode illustrates a broader political dynamic. Rather than tightening obligations on the most exclusionary territories, reforms sometimes focus on “relieving” those that are already absorbing more than their share. The dashboard allows users to test, visually and empirically, what such a shift implies.

To link SRU implementation to broader questions of territorial justice and “moving to opportunity”, this section of the dashboard also introduces a Weighted Bivariate Alignment Index[11]. Rather than measuring the intrinsic quality of dwellings, this index asks whether growth in social‑housing provision has taken place in the right kinds of places: those where social need is highest or where environmental and service conditions are most favorable. For each commune, the method compares the recent change in its share of social housing (over roughly 2012–2022) with seven sets of conditions: income, poverty, ageing, heat exposure, energy performance of the housing stock, green coverage, and access to health facilities. Each of these pairs is treated as a simple bivariate. Both variables are ranked nationally and split into three groups (low / medium / high), producing nine possible combinations. A scoring grid then assigns a score from 0 to 10 to each combination, with one simple rule: the most desirable cell is always the one where social‑housing growth is strongest and the thematic condition points to a strong case for provision (for example, high poverty and high growth, or good access to health services and high growth). After a small orientation step that flips the sign of variables where “low” is desirable (for instance, heat exposure), each commune receives seven such scores (one per theme). These are combined into a weighted average, rescaled to 0–100, with equal weights by default and a minimum coverage rule that avoids computing an index on very sparse data. A high index value therefore means that, across the available themes, social housing has tended to grow where need or opportunity is greatest. A low value indicates the opposite pattern. In the dashboard, this synthetic measure is used alongside raw SRU indicators, not in place of them, to reveal whether, over time, the geography of French social housing has shifted toward territories offering better health and quality‑of‑life prospects for low‑income households, or whether it has mainly deepened existing patterns of concentration.

Technically, the index is computed from a consolidated commune‑level master table that brings together SRU data, INSEE socio‑demographic indicators, environmental metrics and health‑access measures. The goal is to offer a transparent, configurable lens on the question: are we actually locating social housing in places that expand, rather than constrain, the life chances of its residents?

**<u>Four housing regimes: reading SRU through territorial typologies</u>**

To make sense of the uneven geography revealed by SRU data, the dashboard proposes four “housing regimes”, or ideal‑types that recur across the national territory. These regimes offer analytical lenses instead of rigid categories. They help users understand how similar logics can operate in very different places, from the French Riviera to Brittany and the overseas DROM Départements et Régions d’Outre-Mer (Guadeloupe, Martinique, Guyane, La Réunion, Mayotte).

Each regime can be explored through the dashboard’s filters and profiles. Below, I briefly outline the four types, which are developed in more detail in the corresponding sections of the interface. Across all four regimes, an important cross‑cutting point is that “doing” social housing does not always mean pouring concrete on empty land. Acquisitions‑améliorations, ANAH‑conventionned units, and rehabilitation of degraded private stock often play a crucial role, especially in dense or land‑constrained areas. Early evaluations of SRU‑related funds (such as the Fonds d’aménagement urbain) show that, while a majority of credits went to new supply over the past quarter century, a non‑negligible share (around a quarter of the dwellings supported in some regions) came from acquisitions or rehabilitation, with strong regional variations. For instance, parts of PACA French Riviera rely heavily on rehab because new projects are hard to get off the ground, whereas Aquitaine relies more on acquisitions to circumvent this challenge. In the dashboard, these differences appear indirectly, through the timing and localization of stock changes in relation to land constraints and political dynamics.

***1. Typology 1 – Exclusionary French Riviera regimes***

In some of the most affluent coastal communes, especially along the French Riviera, the combination of high land values, intense tourist economies, and entrenched social hierarchies has produced regimes of near‑systematic resistance to social housing. Here, the SRU obligations are often seen not as a shared national responsibility but as an external imposition that threatens a carefully curated local image. In these regimes, the dashboard often shows persistent deficits relative to SRU targets, sometimes over multiple triennial periods, a narrow or skewed mix of social housing categories, with very few PLAI units, and planning strategies that confine such housing as far as possible from prime coastal land or central amenities, when it is built at all.

Financial sanctions under Article 55 are frequently treated as a manageable cost of doing business, instead of a spur to change. Political campaigns by local mayors may explicitly or implicitly promise to “protect” the commune from unwanted newcomers. This is SRU as constant battlefield, with national law pushing in one direction and local land use ideologies pulling in another. Local conflicts around more modest forms of affordable housing, such as baux réels solidaires or mixed‑tenure projects aimed at retaining year‑round residents in highly touristified villages, often illustrate how quickly “densification” and “preservation of character” can be mobilized to block even carefully designed schemes. In some coastal communes where a majority of dwellings are now second homes and school enrolments are falling, referendums or local campaigns have derailed projects intended to keep younger and working‑age households in place, reinforcing the logic of a village‑as‑resort rather than as a lived‑in community.

One recent conflict in Allauch, perched above Marseille, encapsulates this Riviera‑style regime. With only about 7,5% of social housing, the commune is far below the 25% SRU threshold, and the mayor has accumulated roughly 1,3 million euros in fines rather than substantially change course. When the Prefect of the Bouches‑du‑Rhône region moved in March 2025 to pre‑empt a 2,500 m² plot to build around fifteen social housing units[12], the mayor launched an [online petition](https://www.change.org/p/arrêté-préfectoral-et-logements-préservons-le-cadre-de-vie-d-allauch?utm_source=openai), quickly gathering more than 7,000 signatures[13], framing the project as an attack on the “centenary DNA” of a village used as a picturesque set for the soap opera “Plus belle la vie”. In Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur more broadly, the scale of resistance is structural. With around 8% of the French population, the region accounts for roughly a quarter of the national SRU non-compliance, and out of 197 communes subject to SRU obligations, only 14 actually meet the 25% target[14]. Petition comments often revolve around the idea that those who pay high prices for tranquility should not have social housing near them. Regional housing advocates underline how this rests on a distorted image of social tenants, automatically associated with delinquency and degradation, despite the fact that they are simply low‑ or modest‑income households needing support, and that contemporary social housing landlords have significantly improved both the design and management of their buildings over the past 15–20 years.

***2. Typology 2 – Hyper‑proactive but imbalanced metro regions: the Paris case***

Paris is often held up as a SRU success story. In a context of extreme land scarcity and high prices, the city has significantly raised the share of social housing in its overall stock, including in some of its wealthiest arrondissements. From the vantage point of national aggregates, the capital looks like a champion. The dashboard confirms this proactive stance: strong growth in social housing stock, substantial numbers of approvals, and relatively ambitious production of PLAI and PLUS. Yet, a closer, more granular view reveals deep internal imbalances. Social housing is unevenly distributed across neighborhoods and building types. Certain pockets of the city have become dense clusters of precarious households, while other high‑value areas remain comparatively protected. The types of projects and financial arrangements used to deliver social housing can create very different experiences for residents.

Fieldwork in three emblematic Parisian sites illustrates these tensions:

1.  12 rue Jean‑Bart: an interstitial adaptive reuse project, where existing built fabric is re‑purposed to create social housing in a tightly constrained urban environment

2.  La Samaritaine: a high‑profile redevelopment project under a public‑private partnership (PPP) framework, where social housing is embedded within a broader luxury and commercial operation

3.  Tour Bois‑le‑Prêtre: a landmark renovation of an existing social housing tower, combining architectural innovation with questions about who benefits from and who is displaced by such transformations

Through these cases, the “Paris regime” appears both exemplary and ambivalent. It works as the living proof that SRU can be used to push social housing into the heart of a global city, but also as a reminder that quantitative targets can mask significant qualitative and spatial inequalities. Paris also illustrates how the aggressive use of the full SRU toolbox, including the acquisition of private buildings, the rehabilitation of former State sites like old military premises, and the aggressive public financing of these operations, can change who gets to live in the most central and prestigious neighborhoods. In recent years, the city has converted former public offices into hundreds of social dwellings, imposed strict limits on short‑term rentals, and massively expanded public housing so that roughly one in four Parisians now lives in social housing[15]. This has made Paris a reference point in international discussions on social mix, while simultaneously generating long waiting times and strong local resistance in some central arrondissements, where inhabitants contest further social housing or densification. The greater Paris metro region thus condenses the promise and the contradictions of SRU in a single metropolitan setting.

***3. Typology 3 – Balanced builders: Rennes and the Brittany model***

In parts of Brittany, notably around Rennes, a different housing regime emerges. There, communes have combined sustained social housing production with a comparatively balanced territorial distribution and a strong local culture of planning for social mix. This did not happen overnight, and it often took multi-level enforcement, namely from the intermunicipal and regional levels, to push the most exclusionary communes in the region to do their fair share in social housing provision. In these regimes, dashboard indicators often show high or improving compliance with SRU targets, without extreme concentrations of social housing in a few “sacrificial” neighbourhoods, a more evenly spread provision of PLAI and PLUS units across the intercommunal area, and relatively stable or improving socio‑economic and health indicators in neighborhoods with substantial social housing stocks.

At the same time, the “Rennes success story” cannot be read as if Brittany were sheltered from housing pressures. Insee regional statistics underline that the social stock is proportionally smaller here than in many other French regions, even though around 70% of Breton households are eligible for social housing and more than a third of social‑housing tenants live below the poverty line[16]. On the coast and in tourist cities such as Saint‑Malo or Vannes, second homes, short‑term rentals and *meublés de tourisme* have helped drive prices to levels that are increasingly out of reach for local residents, prompting protests and calls for stronger regulation of Airbnb‑style platforms and tighter controls on speculative acquisitions[17]. In other words, Brittany combines a relatively proactive metropolitan core around Rennes with severe housing tensions on the coast. The regional picture is therefore more ambivalent than the Rennes model alone might suggest.

In Brittany, there is still work to do in terms of social housing provision and territorial rebalancing. But the political story is less about resisting national constraints and more about using them as supports for locally devised housing strategies. The Breton cases demonstrate that SRU can be an ally for mayors and planners who already see social mix and access to housing as central to their territorial project. In other words, the Brittany case stands sometimes in contrast with the exclusionary Riviera regimes. Rennes Métropole, for example, has experimented with instruments that go beyond national requirements: a “loyer unique” across its HLM stock, so that a T2 in the city center costs the same as a T2 in a working‑class neighborhood like Maurepas, and a strong push for baux réels solidaires to make homeownership more accessible to a broad range of residents. These local innovations, combined with a deliberate strategy to align national tools like SRU with metropolitan goals, show how a territory can try to prevent the classic pattern of affluent cores and relegated peripheries, even under intense market pressures.

***4. Typology 4 – Over‑burdened and under‑resourced: the DROM territories***

Finally, in the overseas départements et régions d’outre‑mer (DROM), SRU interacts with a very specific historical and socio‑economic context. These territories are characterized by high levels of poverty, chronic under‑investment in basic infrastructure, and housing markets shaped by legacies of colonialism and structural dependency. Their economies were long organized as plantation or extractive peripheries for the European métropole, with land ownership, urban planning and labor markets structured to serve export sectors rather than local needs. Even without a full break in political status after formal decolonization, these territories remain heavily dependent on transfers and decisions made in Paris, fragile local tax bases, concentrated landholdings, and segregated urban forms in which low‑income, racialized populations are often confined to the most vulnerable housing and neighborhoods.

The dashboard allows users to compare DROM communes with metropolitan ones using the same SRU indicators (targets, stocks, outcomes) but this formal equivalence can be misleading. In many DROM territories, the constraints are incomparable. These territories are faced with rapid demographic growth and overcrowding, limited fiscal and administrative capacity at local level, and a much stronger vulnerability to environmental risks and climate change, which further complicate land use and housing policy. Yet, SRU obligations and national financing frameworks frequently apply without fully accounting for these structural differences. The result is a regime where communes are asked to deliver on social housing targets with far fewer tools and resources than those available in wealthier metropolitan regions. These territories highlight, in an extreme form, the limits of a “one size fits all” implementation of SRU and the urgency of rethinking how territorial justice is operationalized.

The difficulty to collect data in these territories makes this call for more contextual approaches to SRU implementation there even more important. While census data and the RPLS provide a relatively robust picture for Guadeloupe, Martinique, Guyane, La Réunion and Mayotte, information is far patchier for smaller COM like Wallis‑et‑Futuna, Saint‑Barthélemy or Saint‑Pierre‑et‑Miquelon, where “HLM loué vide” categories do not capture the full range of social providers, and local open‑data systems are incomplete or inconsistent. Building a comprehensive SRU‑style view of social housing in these territories has therefore required painstaking assembly from scattered Insee files, ministerial reports, and EU‑level documents. This is another reminder that territorial justice begins with the capacity to see clearly what is happening everywhere, including in the smallest and most remote territories.

From the supply side, the DROM have continued to see relatively dynamic growth in their social stock: between 2022 and 2023, the number of social rental units there increased by about 1.6%, compared with 1.0% nationally[18], and territories like La Réunion have expanded their subsidized rental stock by more than 16% over the last decade. In 2023, social landlords in the DROM delivered just under 3,000 new rental units and funded roughly the same number, while also financing close to 4,000 rehabilitations (about 3,000 in the social stock). Yet, official estimates put current needs at around 90,000 dwellings for the DROM alone (and about 110,000 when including the COM). These numbers encompass new social and very social rentals, affordable ownership, and the eradication of substandard housing. Even in years of strong mobilization, annual new‑build output remains a fraction of this backlog, with average delivery times for projects stretching to four years or more due to limited land availability, complex procedures, and fragile local construction sectors.

Broader socio‑economic indicators reinforce this picture. In 2021, the poverty rate in the DROM ranged from roughly a third to more than three quarters of the population. Around 34–35% of people in Guadeloupe, about 27% in Martinique, 36% in La Réunion and more than 50% in Guyane lived below the national poverty threshold, while around 77% of residents in Mayotte were below that same threshold, compared to about 14–15% in mainland France[19]. At the same time, average social rents per square metre in the DROM (typically between about 5.9 and 6.6 €/m² in departments like Guadeloupe, close to the national social‑housing average of 6.27 €/m² in 2023) are not dramatically lower than metropolitan levels, despite significantly lower average incomes and higher living costs (for example, food and energy prices 6–10% above the Hexagon in some territories). The combination of very low incomes and relatively high housing costs makes the question of allocations, waiting times, and arrears structurally sharper than in most metropolitan regions.

Financing arrangements also differ in overseas DROM territories. Social housing there largely depends on a dedicated budget line, or Ligne Budgétaire Unique (LBU), managed by the Ministry for Overseas Territories, complemented by a specific tax credit, Action Logement contributions, and long‑term loans from the Banque des Territoires[20]. While nominal authorizations under the LBU have increased in recent years (with, for example, a roughly 20% rise in commitments in the 2024 finance bill), actual disbursements are often constrained by administrative bottlenecks and the difficulty of assembling viable projects. In practice, this can mean that overseas territories, which face the highest rates of poverty and the strongest exposure to climate risks, are also those where the pipeline of social housing projects is most fragile and where the underutilization of national credits coexists with massive unmet needs.

**Using the dashboard**

Across these four regimes, and the many variations between them, the dashboard offers a common set of tools. Users can:

-   Look up the profile of any commune and see its SRU history over twenty‑five years;

-   Map and compare patterns of compliance;

-   Overlay political, socio‑economic, and health indicators to understand what is at stake when some territories build and others refuse;

-   Explore the typologies and case studies to connect abstract numbers to concrete local contexts.

By making the “black box” of SRU data transparent and navigable, the project aims to equip policymakers, researchers, journalists, housing activists, and residents with evidence they can mobilize. This election year, at a time when right-wing and extreme-right-wing reform propositions have shown how easily the spirit of SRU can be diluted, this work argues that the way forward is not to cap efforts where they are most advanced, but to demand more from those communes that have so far treated social housing as somebody else’s problem. It also suggests that robust, shared housing data infrastructure, maintained over time, embedded either in universities or in public institutions, and accessible to local actors, is itself a condition for any serious enforcement of fair‑share obligations.
